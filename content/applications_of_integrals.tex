\section{Applications of Integrals}

Many applications of integrals use the following heuristic idea: you have a value $A$ that you want to compute; you break the problem up into infinitesimal pieces and compute the corresponding infinitesimal value ''$dA$''; then sum up the small values by integrating
$$A=\int\ dA.$$

Understanding integrals as sums will make understanding these integral applications easier.
The first example of this you will see is computing areas. \mar{Try to find all such examples.}

\subsection{Areas}
Suppose $f(x)\geq g(x)$ for every $x\in[a,b]$, so that $f$ is the upper curve and $g$ is the lower curve. To compute the area $A$ between them, imagine dividing the region into infinitesimally small strips, each with height $f(x)-g(x)$ and (infinitesimal) width $dx$. The area of the strip is therefore $dA=(f(x)-g(x))\ dx$. One such strip is shown on the graph below (the width enlarged to be visible). To compute the desired area, simply integrate $dA$ over $[a,b]$:
$$A=\int dA = \int_a^b(f(x)-g(x))\ dx.$$

\subsection{Arc Length}

The \textbf{arc length} of $f(x)$ on $[a,b]$ is the length of string needed to trace the graph of $f$ on the interval $[a,b]$. Similarly to computing areas, to compute the arc length $L$ of a function $f$'s graph, divide the length into infinitesimally short segments (that we assume to be linear). Then each segment's length can be computed using the pythagorean theorem (see picture below):
$$dL=\sqrt{dx^2+dy^2}=\sqrt{1+\frac{dy^2}{dx^2}}\ dx=\sqrt{1+f'(x)^2}\ dx.$$
Then to compute the arc length $L$, sum $dL$ over $[a,b]$ by integrating:
$$L=\int_a^b\sqrt{1+f'(x)^2}\ dx.$$

\subsection{Volumes and Surface Area}

The same idea we used for area works for volume: cut a solid into thin
pieces, find the volume of one piece, and add them up.

\subsubsection{Cross Sections}

Suppose a solid extends from $x=a$ to $x=b$, and a slice perpendicular to
the $x$-axis has area $A(x)$. If $A$ is continuous, a thin slice of width
$dx$ has volume $dV=A(x)\ dx$, so
$$V=\int_a^b A(x)\ dx.$$
The important thing is to find the \textit{area} of each cross section,
not just its height. The slices might be squares, circles, triangles,
or something else.

\subsubsection{Disks and Washers}

A \textbf{solid of revolution} is made by rotating a plane region around
a line. If we rotate the region under a nonnegative function $f$ around
the $x$-axis, each vertical slice becomes a disk of radius $f(x)$. Thus
$$dV=\pi f(x)^2\ dx
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
V=\pi\int_a^b f(x)^2\ dx.$$

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\input{content/figures/solid_disk_slice.tex}
\caption{Rotating the region under $y=x$ on $[0,1]$ around the $x$-axis makes a cone. The blue slice is a disk with thickness $dx$.}
\label{fig:disk-slice}
\end{figure}

If there is a hole, the cross section is a \textbf{washer}: a big disk
with a smaller disk removed. Let $R(x)$ be the outer radius and $r(x)$
the inner radius, with $R(x)\geq r(x)\geq0$. Then
$$V=\pi\int_a^b\bigl(R(x)^2-r(x)^2\bigr)\ dx.$$
Both radii are distances from the axis of rotation. Notice that we
subtract the \textit{squares} of the radii, rather than squaring their
difference.

\begin{exampleblock}[breakable]
Rotate the region in Figure~\ref{fig:volume-base} around the $x$-axis.
The outer radius is $R(x)=x$, and the inner radius is $r(x)=x^2$.
\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\input{content/figures/solid_washer_slice.tex}
\caption{Figure~\ref{fig:volume-base} rotated around the $x$-axis, with part cut away to show a blue washer of thickness $dx$.}
\label{fig:washer-slice}
\end{figure}

Therefore,
$$\begin{aligned}
V&=\pi\int_0^1(x^2-x^4)\ dx\\
 &=\pi\left[\frac{x^3}{3}-\frac{x^5}{5}\right]_0^1
 =\frac{2\pi}{15}.
\end{aligned}$$
\end{exampleblock}

\subsubsection{Cylindrical Shells}

Sometimes it is easier to use slices \textit{parallel} to the axis of
rotation. A vertical strip rotated around the $y$-axis makes a thin
\textbf{cylindrical shell}. Its volume is approximately its circumference
times its height times its thickness:
$$dV=2\pi(\text{radius})(\text{height})\ dx.$$

Suppose $f(x)\geq g(x)$ on $[a,b]$, with $0\leq a<b$. Rotating the region
between them around the $y$-axis gives shells of radius $x$ and height
$f(x)-g(x)$, so
$$V=2\pi\int_a^b x\bigl(f(x)-g(x)\bigr)\ dx.$$
For washers, slice perpendicular to the axis. For shells, slice parallel
to it. Horizontal slices work the same way, using $dy$.

\begin{exampleblock}[breakable]
Now rotate the region in Figure~\ref{fig:volume-base} around the $y$-axis.
\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\input{content/figures/solid_shell_slice.tex}
\caption{Figure~\ref{fig:volume-base} rotated around the $y$-axis, with part cut away to show a blue shell of radial thickness $dx$.}
\label{fig:cylindrical-shell}
\end{figure}

Each shell has radius $x$ and height $x-x^2$, so
$$\begin{aligned}
V&=2\pi\int_0^1x(x-x^2)\ dx\\
 &=2\pi\left(\frac13-\frac14\right)=\frac{\pi}{6}.
\end{aligned}$$
The axis changed, so this is a different solid from the washer example.
\end{exampleblock}

\subsubsection{Surface Area}

To find the area of a surface of revolution, use the arc length idea
from earlier. A short piece of the curve, of length $dL$, sweeps out a
thin band. Its area is circumference times slanted length:
$$dS=2\pi(\text{radius})\ dL.$$

If $f\geq0$ has a continuous derivative on $[a,b]$, rotating its graph
around the $x$-axis gives
$$S=2\pi\int_a^b f(x)\sqrt{1+f'(x)^2}\ dx.$$
Here $f(x)$ is the radius, and $\sqrt{1+f'(x)^2}\ dx$ is the arc length
piece. Around the $y$-axis, when $x\geq0$, replace the radius $f(x)$ by
$x$. These formulas give the surface swept out by the curve; add any
flat end caps separately.

\begin{exampleblock}
Rotate $y=x$ on $[0,1]$ around the $x$-axis. Since $f'(x)=1$,
$$S=2\pi\int_0^1x\sqrt{1+1^2}\ dx=\pi\sqrt2.$$
This is the slanted surface of a cone. To include its circular base,
add $\pi$, giving total surface area $\pi(1+\sqrt2)$.
\end{exampleblock}

\subsection{Work}

If a constant force moves an object in the direction of the force, the
\textbf{work} done is force times distance. What if the force changes
as the object moves? Break the motion into short distances, just as we
broke a region into thin strips.

Suppose the object moves along a line from $x=a$ to $x=b$, with $a<b$,
and let $F(x)$ be the component of the force in the direction of
increasing $x$. Over a small displacement $dx$, the work is
$dW=F(x)\ dx$. Adding these contributions gives
$$W=\int_a^b F(x)\ dx.$$
A force acting with the motion does positive work; a force opposing it
does negative work. For motion in the other direction, reverse the
bounds. When force is measured in newtons and distance in meters, work
is measured in \textbf{joules}: one joule is one newton-meter.

\subsubsection{Stretching a Spring}

A spring gets harder to stretch as we pull it farther from its natural
length. \textbf{Hooke's law} says that the force needed to stretch it
slowly by a distance $x\geq0$ is $F(x)=kx$, where $k>0$ measures the
stiffness of the spring. The spring itself pulls in the opposite
direction, with force $-kx$.

\begin{exampleblock}
Suppose it takes 10 newtons to hold a spring stretched 0.2 meters beyond
its natural length. Then
$$10=k(0.2)\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad k=50.$$
To stretch it from 0.2 meters to 0.5 meters beyond its natural length,
we do work
$$\begin{aligned}
W&=\int_{0.2}^{0.5}50x\ dx
  =\left[25x^2\right]_{0.2}^{0.5}\\
 &=25(0.25-0.04)=5.25\text{ joules}.
\end{aligned}$$
We start the integral at 0.2 because the spring is already stretched
that far.
\end{exampleblock}

\subsubsection{Pumping Water}

When we pump water out of a tank, different layers travel different
distances. Near Earth's surface, a mass $m$ has weight $mg$, where
$g\approx9.8$ meters per second squared. If a thin layer has volume
$dV$ and the fluid has mass density $\rho$, its weight is $\rho g\ dV$.
Therefore,
$$dW=(\text{weight of a layer})(\text{distance it is lifted}).$$
Find these two quantities before setting up the integral.

\begin{exampleblock}[breakable]
A cylindrical tank of radius 1 meter and depth 2 meters is full of
water. How much work is needed to empty it through an outlet 3 meters
above the bottom? Ignore friction and changes in kinetic energy, and
use water density $\rho=1000$ kilograms per cubic meter.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\input{content/figures/pumping_water.tex}
\caption{A layer at height $y$ must be lifted $3-y$ meters.}
\label{fig:pumping-water}
\end{figure}

Measure $y$ upward from the bottom. A horizontal layer has volume
$dV=\pi(1)^2\ dy=\pi\ dy$, so its weight is $9800\pi\ dy$ newtons.
Its lifting distance is $3-y$, and the water occupies $0\leq y\leq2$.
Thus
$$\begin{aligned}
W&=\int_0^2 9800\pi(3-y)\ dy\\
 &=9800\pi\left[3y-\frac{y^2}{2}\right]_0^2\\
 &=39200\pi\text{ joules}.
\end{aligned}$$
The lifting distance depends on the layer's height, even though every
layer has the same cross-sectional area.
\end{exampleblock}

\subsection{Probability}

Imagine we have an experiment, and every time it is performed the outcome is a real number. (Think throwing a dart at the plane, and the outcome is the $x$ coordinate of the point it lands on).

To start, the we will define an \textbf{event} to be a subset of $\R$. Given such a subset $A\subset\R$, our goal is to describe the probability that the outcome $X$ is in $A$. We denote this $\P(X\in A)$.


For the kinds of experiments we will consider, we will assume that it is never possible to get the same outcome twice. This means that the probability of any \textit{particular} outcome is 0: for any $a\in\R$, $$\P(X\in\{a\})=\P(X=a)=0.$$
This might seem paradoxical, because the dart must hit \textit{some} point. However, it should make sense since there are infinitely many possible outcomes.
\mar{Think about this carefully.}

To compute $\P(X\in A)$ in general, we need a way to describe an allocation of probability specific to the experiment that is happening. For this (of course) we use a function! A bounded function $f:\R \to \R$ is called a \textbf{probability density function} (PDF) if it is non-negative and $\int_{-\infty}^\infty f(x)\ dx= 1$.\mar{What does the condition $$\int_{-\infty}^\infty f(x)\ dx= 1$$ mean in terms of probability?}
To compute a probability, we just have to integrate:
$$\P(X\in A) = \int_A f(x)\ dx.$$
This notation means that if $A$ is the union of disjoint intervals, integrate over each interval and add the results. In particular, if $A=(a,b)$, then
$$\P(X\in A) = \int_a^b f(x)\ dx.$$
The anti-derivative function
$$F(x)=\P(X<x) = \int_{-\infty}^x f(t)\ dt$$
is called the \textbf{cumulative distribution function} (CDF).

Two statistics often used in probability are the \textbf{expected value} and the \textbf{variance}. The expected value of a PDF (if it exists) is the average value of an outcome. In finite cases, we compute the average of a finite data set with $n$ elements
$$\{\underbrace{x_1,\dots, x_1}_{a_1}, \underbrace{x_2,\dots, x_2}_{a_2},\dots, \underbrace{x_k,\dots, x_k}_{a_k}\}$$
by computing
$$\mu=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^ka_ix_i=\sum_{i=1}^k\frac{a_i}{n}x_i =\sum_{i=1}^k\P(x_i)x_i,$$
the sum of the unique elements, each multiplied by its probability of occurring in the data set. In the continuous case we analogously compute the expected value as
$$\mu=\int_{-\infty}^\infty xf(x)\ dx.$$

The variance of a PDF (if it exists) is the average squared distance of an outcome from the expected value. Therefore the variance is computes as
$$\sigma^2 = \int_{-\infty}^\infty (x-\mu)^2f(x)\ dx.$$
The square root of the variance is called the \textbf{standard deviation}.

\begin{exampleblock}[breakable]
The ``uniform'' PDF on $(0,1)$ is defined as
$$f(x)=\begin{cases}0 & x < 0\\ 1 & 0\leq x\leq 1\\ 0 & x>1\end{cases}
\quad\quad\text{ and } \quad\quad
F(x)=\begin{cases}0 & x < 0\\ x & 0\leq x\leq 1\\ 1 & x>1\end{cases}$$
is its CDF. The probability that an outcome of an experiment governed by this PDF is greater than $1/4$ is
$$\P\left(X\in(1/4,\infty)\right)
= \int_{\frac14}^{\infty}f(x)\ dx
= \int_{\frac14}^{1}1\ dx + \int_{1}^{\infty}0\ dx
= \frac34.
$$
The probability that an outcome is in $(0,1/2)\cup(2/3,1)$ is
$$\P\left(X\in(0,1/2)\cup(2/3,1)\right)
= \int_0^{\frac12}1\ dx + \int_{\frac23}^11\ dx
= \frac12+\frac13 = \frac56.
$$

The expected value of the uniform PDF is
$$\mu=\int_{0}^1 x\ dx=\frac{1}{2},$$
and the variance is
$$\sigma^2 = \int_{0}^1 \left(x-\frac{1}{2}\right)^2\ dx=\frac{1}{12}.$$
\end{exampleblock}

\begin{exampleblock}
The ``exponential'' PDF is defined as
$$f(x)=\begin{cases}0 & x < 0\\ e^{-x} & x\geq 0\end{cases}
\quad\quad\text{ and } \quad\quad
F(x)=\begin{cases}0 & x < 0\\1- e^{-x} & x\geq 0\end{cases}$$
is its CDF. This PDF is often used to model the wait time between phone calls. The probability that an outcome of an experiment governed by this PDF is greater than $1/4$ is
$$\P\left(X\in(1/4,\infty)\right)
= \int_{\frac14}^{\infty} e^{-x}\ dx
= e^{-\frac{1}{4}}.
$$
The expected value of the exponential PDF is
$$\mu=\int_{0}^\infty xe^{-x}\ dx=1,$$
and the variance is
$$\sigma^2 = \int_{0}^\infty \left(x-1\right)^2e^{-x}\ dx=1.$$

\end{exampleblock}
